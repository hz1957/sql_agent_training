Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct · Pure SFT vs. Mixed SFT

A Controlled Comparison of Single-Turn Supervision and Trajectory-Mixed Supervision

Experiment log

Abstract

This study compares parameter-efficient supervised fine-tuning strategies starting from the same base Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct, with a focus on the role of execution-verified trajectories in the SQL agent protocol. Step one directly compares Pure-SFT and Mixed-SFT: on the same set of 500 Spider validation samples, Pure-SFT checkpoint-300 reaches an execution accuracy of 77.60%, versus 80.60% for Mixed-SFT checkpoint-300, an absolute difference of +3.00 percentage points. Step two fixes the Mixed-SFT data and sweeps a learning-rate / LoRA-rank grid; lr=5e-5, r=32 remains the top point estimate, and expanding to r=64 brings no stable gain. Step three fixes lr=5e-5, r=32, 4,800 training records, and 300 optimizer steps, and compares the ratio of gold SQL to trajectory supervision; 3,200 gold + 1,600 trajectory achieves the highest execution accuracy at 79.80%, a +3.40 percentage-point improvement over the 4,800 gold-only condition's 76.40%. Overall, the results support mixing roughly one third execution-verified trajectories into single-turn gold-SQL supervision, though the current conclusions still rest on a single training seed and a 500-sample validation subset.

Research Questions

This experiment aims to answer three questions. First, under the SQL agent's execution-feedback evaluation protocol, does introducing execution-verified trajectory supervision yield an observable execution-accuracy gain over Pure-SFT — which uses only single-turn question/schema-to-SQL samples — once the model starting point, LoRA capacity, learning rate, training budget, and evaluation generation settings are held constant? Second, once Mixed-SFT has shown a positive point estimate, does a limited grid over learning rate and LoRA rank change the leading candidate configuration? Third, under a fixed total number of training records and optimization budget, how does the mixing ratio of gold SQL to trajectory supervision affect final execution accuracy, rewrite behavior, and the rate of turn-budget exhaustion?

1. Step One: Pure-SFT vs. Mixed-SFT

The core hypothesis tested in step one is whether, given the same 14B base checkpoint, LoRA capacity, learning rate, global batch size, and 300-step training budget, replacing part of the single-turn gold SQL with execution-verified agent trajectories improves the model's final SQL correctness under the multi-turn execution-feedback protocol. The original 14B model without LoRA is used only to characterize pre-finetuning performance; the primary effect size for step one is defined as the paired execution-accuracy difference between Mixed-SFT checkpoint-300 and Pure-SFT checkpoint-300.

3.4 SFT Conclusions

The overall conclusions of the SFT experiments are as follows. First, Mixed-SFT's point-estimate improvement over Pure-SFT replicates across two independent setups: step one's 3,200 gold + 1,600 trajectory reaches 80.60%, and the same ratio in step three, after regenerating the trajectory pool, reaches 79.80% — both above their respective gold-only controls. Second, lr=5e-5, r=32 is currently the best and simplest LoRA configuration; r=64 has not shown itself to be necessary. Third, under a fixed total number of training records, roughly one third trajectory supervision is currently the best ratio; it outperforms pure gold-only while more reliably lowering the average number of turns and the turn-limit-exhaustion rate than 50% trajectory does.

Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct-SFT-Merged

GRPO Reward Design and Search Strategy Experiment Results

Experiment log

Abstract

This experiment studies two-stage parameter-efficient training: first, the SFT LoRA for Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct is merged into the 14B base to form a unified SFT-Merged policy; a new LoRA is then trained for each GRPO scheme on top of that policy, rather than updating all 14B parameters. In the initial S1–S4 reward-design comparison, S3 has the highest execution-accuracy point estimate at 80.67%, a 3.33-percentage-point improvement over the SFT-Merged baseline; the subsequent hyperparameter experiment settled on KL=0.01, PPO epoch=2, temperature=1.0, and gamma=0.9. On the updated SFT ratio base, the rollout comparison yields 83.67% for S3 branch=4/beam=2/n=20, 83.33% for S1 n=8, and 82.00% for S3 branch=8/beam=1/n=24; accuracy does not separate clearly, but S1 n=8 has the best compute and interaction efficiency.

Research Questions

This experiment aims to answer: under the same SFT-Merged base policy and the same second-stage LoRA training framework, do different reward designs and rollout search strategies provide an additional execution-accuracy gain over the SFT-Merged baseline; and how should differences among S1–S4 be interpreted given that they coexist with potential confounders such as the reward variable, chain/tree rollout form, batch size, and PPO mini-batch size.

1. Method

This section describes the objects of comparison, controlled variables, and evaluation protocol for the GRPO experiments. To avoid conflating the first-stage supervised fine-tuning with the second-stage reinforcement learning, the report separately describes the construction of the SFT policy, the training of the GRPO adapters, and the final agent evaluation.

S1–S4 in this document are code names for four GRPO schemes, each jointly denoting a rollout structure and a reward design. S1 denotes chain rollout + final execution reward, corresponding to chain_final; S2 adds an executable fallback for failed paths on top of S1, corresponding to chain_executable; S3 denotes tree rollout + final execution reward with tree backup, corresponding to tree_final; S4 adds an executable fallback on top of S3, corresponding to tree_executable. The chain schemes sample multiple independent multi-turn trajectories from the same prompt and estimate advantage within the group; the tree schemes branch and prune at every SQL action node and back up the value of leaves or subtrees to the parent node. The executable fallback's beta=0.1 acts, in the tree schemes, as an auxiliary advantage weight for the failed group, not as a floor on the raw value.

1.1 Model Lineage and Two-Stage LoRA

Stage one initializes a supervised fine-tuning policy from Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct. All subsequent GRPO experiments uniformly use the same SFT checkpoint as their starting point; this checkpoint corresponds to LoRA rank 32, alpha 64, learning rate 5e-5, at global step 300 of training. The key control point here is that S1–S4 do not start from different SFT starting points, but share the same, already-finetuned SFT policy.

To let the second-stage training compare only the differences intrinsic to GRPO, the selected SFT adapter is first merged into the 14B base, forming a unified Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct-SFT-Merged policy. The tokenizer remains identical to the base 14B model. SFT-Merged can therefore be treated as a fixed initial policy that is "14B base + SFT LoRA effect"; once GRPO begins, the stage-one SFT adapter is not reloaded.

Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct + SFT LoRA (r=32, alpha=64, checkpoint-300) → fixed SFT-Merged policy + fresh GRPO LoRA (r=64, alpha=128, all-linear) → S1 / S2 / S3 / S4 LoRA checkpoint

Stage two trains a separate new actor LoRA for each GRPO scheme, with rank 64, alpha 128, target modules all-linear, and a learning rate that remains 5e-5. S1–S4 therefore compare "different GRPO adapters trained on the same SFT policy," not four different full-parameter models.

At evaluation time, the SFT-Merged baseline loads only the fixed SFT policy; S1–S4 each attach their own second-stage GRPO adapter on top of the same SFT policy. This lets the performance differences reported here be attributed to the effect of policy updates during the GRPO stage, while avoiding the introduction of stage-one SFT weight differences into the comparison.

1.2 SFT/GRPO Data Isolation and the Formal Training Run's Scale

The final formal S1 chain training run isolates SFT and GRPO data by source_uid. The original Spider train pool contains 7,000 questions; the selected Mixed-SFT dataset contains 4,800 training records — 3,200 gold, 1,137 direct trajectory, and 463 rewrite trajectory — corresponding to 4,533 unique source questions. The training record count exceeds the unique question count because a single source question can contribute multiple trajectory records.

When constructing the formal GRPO data, the training pool excludes all 4,533 SFT source_uids, yielding 2,467 GRPO prompts, for an SFT/GRPO overlap of 0 as measured by source question[1]. The formal run completed 275 steps with a train batch size of 8, for a cumulative total of 2,200 prompt presentations; each prompt is configured with rollout_n=8, giving 17,600 rollout slots. It is important to distinguish the data pool size from the training budget here: the available GRPO pool has 2,467 records, while the actual optimization process consumed 2,200 prompt instances cumulatively; SFT's 4,800, meanwhile, is the training record count, corresponding to 4,533 unique questions.

The zero-overlap conclusion above applies specifically to the final 275-step formal S1 training run. The earlier short-run experiments used to select reward, KL, temperature, gamma, and rollout scheme drew on the full Spider train pool and should not be described as using the same data-isolation setup.

1.4 Reward and the Executable Fallback

Algorithmic semantics should be distinguished from code identifiers: the configuration name chain_executable denotes "chain final + executable fallback," and tree_executable denotes "tree final + executable fallback." Both S2 and S4 retain the final correctness reward and its discounted backup; the difference is that S2 writes executability into the raw score of failed trajectories, whereas S4 uses executability only as a normalized fallback advantage signal when a tree sibling group shows no final-correctness distinction whatsoever.

In chain rollout, the verifier only issues an outcome reward RT when a trajectory terminates. If the SQL generated at turn T is semantically correct, then RT=1; the empirical return for the action at turn t is obtained by discounting that terminal reward backward, rather than treating the discounted value as an immediate reward given by the environment at that turn. If the entire trajectory ultimately fails, all action returns are 0 under S1; S2, in contrast, assigns a fallback score of beta=0.1 only to SQL actions that execute safely and without error in SQLite. "Executable" here means only execution.ok=True; it does not mean the SQL is semantically equivalent to the gold query.

R_T = 1, if the final SQL is correct; otherwise 0 Q_hat(t) = gamma^(T − t) × R_T, if the trajectory ultimately succeeds Q_hat(t) = beta, if the trajectory ultimately fails and the current SQL is executable (S2 only) Q_hat(t) = 0, otherwise

In chain S2, the executable fallback is a floor that replaces the failure reward, not an additional bonus. So as long as the trajectory ultimately succeeds, S2 uses the same discounted correctness reward as S1, without adding beta on top. This avoids giving a trajectory that "first produces incorrect but executable SQL, then successfully corrects it" a higher total return than one that gets it right on the first turn, simply by accumulating fallback score.

In tree rollout, a correct node is a terminal node whose outcome reward is 1, so that node's correctness-backed return is likewise 1. S3/S4 both first independently compute F(n), which is backed up from final correctness: a non-correct internal node takes the mean of its children's F and multiplies by gamma, and a failed leaf node is 0. S4 no longer takes max(F, beta × I_executable) node by node; the executable fallback is used only to break ties with a normalized executability metric when a sibling group's final-correctness signal cannot distinguish nodes at all.

F(n) = 1, if the current SQL is correct F(n) = gamma × mean[F(c)], for a non-correct internal node F(n) = 0, for a failed leaf node A_i = Normalize(F_i), if range(F) > tau A_i = beta × Normalize(I_executable(i)), if max(F)=0 and executability differs across the group (S4 only) A_i = 0, otherwise

Chain and tree therefore use the same class of terminal-reward discounted propagation; the difference is that chain propagates along a single sampled trajectory, whereas tree averages the returns of multiple sampled child branches at each internal node. There is no learned critic here, and this is not GAE; gamma is used to construct the empirical action returns compared within a GRPO group. Sibling nodes generated from the same parent state form one GRPO comparison group, and node returns are centered and normalized within the group to obtain the advantage. The tree scheme thus compares "the relative returns of different SQL actions under the same database state and feedback."

From the perspective of the literature, training GRPO with only the final verifier reward is a reasonable outcome-supervision variant: DeepSeekMath's GRPO normalizes the final rewards of multiple outputs under the same prompt within the group, and assigns the resulting advantage to that output's tokens[2]. With a binary correct/incorrect reward and a clear split of positive and negative samples within the group — especially at G=2 — this objective is very close to online DPO / contrastive learning; but ordinary GRPO still includes on-policy sampling, group normalization, and PPO clip/KL mechanisms, so it cannot simply be treated as strictly equivalent to DPO[3].

DeepSeekMath presents both outcome-supervised and process-supervised GRPO. The former shows that a final reward is sufficient to define a valid GRPO training objective, but its ablation results show that, for the corresponding math tasks, process supervision outperforms outcome supervision[2]. This result therefore supports using the final verifier reward as a simple, reproducible baseline, but does not support the claim that it is superior to process reward.

DeepSeek-R1 addresses a different dimension: the source of the reward. On verifiable tasks such as math and code, a rule-based programmatic verifier avoids the need to train an additional neural reward model and the reward-hacking risk that comes with it[4]. Neural reward models here include both outcome reward models (ORMs), which score only the complete output, and process reward models (PRMs), which score intermediate reasoning steps; the choice between a rule-based verifier and a neural reward model is not equivalent to the choice between outcome supervision and process supervision.

This experiment uses a programmatic SQL verifier to produce the terminal correctness reward, and then performs credit assignment via chain discounting or tree backup, without introducing a separate process reward model. The final SQL execution reward is therefore a low-complexity, auditable baseline, not a reward design already proven optimal; whether process-level reward can further improve training still requires a separate ablation. In addition, a binary verifier reward can make an entire group correct or entirely incorrect, so within-group reward variance must be monitored to avoid the GRPO advantage degenerating to zero.

1.5 Tree Frontier and Beam Selection

The tree's root state first samples branch_n=4 SQL candidates. Nodes that pass the checker and are executable terminate immediately under prune_on_terminal_proxy=True; nodes with unrecoverable SQLite errors are also pruned as failed leaves, including hallucinated table names that clearly do not exist and are not a minor spelling variant of a schema table name, timeouts, and memory-limit errors. Only nodes that were not terminated by the proxy, have not reached the maximum number of turns, and can construct a rewrite state proceed to the next-layer frontier. When the frontier exceeds beam_size=2, the beam is not chosen as the top 2 by model log probability, nor ranked by the node's final gold reward; instead a proxy score is computed from execution signals visible to the agent during the rollout:

proxy(n) = +0.2 if the SQL is successfully parsed; otherwise −2.0 +1.0 if the SQL is executable; otherwise −0.5 +0.2 if the SQL is executable and its result is not an empty set

This experiment sets beam_tau=1.0 and beam_epsilon_random=0.1. At each selection step, with 10% probability 2 nodes are retained by uniform random sampling without replacement from the frontier; in the remaining 90% of cases, nodes are sampled by weighted, without-replacement sampling according to exp(proxy(n) / tau), rather than deterministically choosing the two highest-scoring nodes. If the frontier has 2 or fewer nodes, all are retained. The random numbers are derived from the task's root_uid, so beam sampling is reproducible given identical frontier contents and order.

Each of the 2 retained parent nodes per layer continues to generate 4 branches. The maximum number of rollout slots for a three-turn tree search is therefore 4 + (3 − 1) × 2 × 4 = 20: 4 nodes in the first turn, and up to 8 nodes in each of the second and third turns.

5. Rollout Scale and Search Structure Comparison

All three formal training runs start from Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct-SFT-Ratio-Gold3200-D1137-R463-LR5e5-R32-Merged, fixing train batch size=4, KL=0.01, PPO epoch=2, temperature=1.0, gamma=0.9, dynamic batching, remove padding, layered summon, number of training steps, and random seed, and are evaluated using the same validation subset and greedy agent protocol. The two S3 experiments change branch, beam, and rollout-slot count simultaneously; S1 also changes the chain/tree rollout, reward scheme, and advantage estimator, so what is compared here is the complete scheme, not the effect of any single parameter in isolation.

Training and inference should first be distinguished: chain is not an outdated scheme but a strong baseline in current GRPO/RLVR and CoT reasoning. Both DeepSeekMath's and DeepSeek-R1's GRPO adopt the chain-output unit of "sample multiple complete outputs for the same question and estimate advantage with within-group reward"; CoT prompting, self-consistency, and best-of-N inference likewise all sample, vote, or rerank over multiple independent reasoning chains. Chain's engineering advantages are simplicity, easy parallelism, direct alignment with the final-answer verifier or SQL execution reward, and no additional bias from a beam/proxy score. Under this experiment's setting — max_turns=3, a verifiable final SQL, and evaluation using the greedy agent protocol — S1 chain n=8 becoming the default candidate is therefore supported by both the literature and engineering considerations.

The tree-based GRPO/agent-RL literature supports a different direction: when a task is long-horizon, multi-hop, involves expensive tool calls, or has high uncertainty in intermediate decisions, organizing a shared prefix into a tree lets the common prefix be reused under a fixed token or tool-call budget, and lets the leaf node's final reward be backed up into branch-, step-, or token-level advantage. Tree-GRPO also shows that intra-tree GRPO and step-level DPO share the same preference-gradient structure. The main theoretical benefit of tree is thus not that "more sampling is necessarily more accurate," but better use of the exploration budget and better credit assignment; these benefits, however, depend on task length, branch position, the advantage estimator, and the rollout budget. This experiment's S3 tree-final is an engineering-oriented tree-rollout variant for the SQL agent, and is not equivalent to the full intra-tree/inter-tree advantage estimator of ICLR 2026 Tree-GRPO.

5. Conclusions and Limitations

The 500-sample paired results support two restrained conclusions. First, S3 shows 29 instances of "incorrect became correct" and 16 of "correct became incorrect" relative to SFT, but the exact McNemar test has not yet crossed the 0.05 threshold. Second, S3 and S1 have similar exclusive-correct counts, showing no stable strategy difference; combined with the main experiment's resource results, S1 remains the cheaper default scheme.

The error cases further show that the most valuable improvement for the next stage is not simply scaling up rollout, but improving checker precision and trajectory state management: prematurely accepting incorrect SQL, repeatedly rejecting correct SQL, and the lack of a best-so-far rollback all erode SQL capability the policy already has. The scope of this page's analysis is limited to the final output and the selected trajectory, and does not further attribute causes within tree search's internal candidate-branch process.

4.1 Problem

In the rollout comparison experiment with fixed 4×H100, train batch size=4, KL=0.01, PPO epoch=2, dynamic batching, remove padding, and layered summon, both S3 branch=4/beam=2/rollout_n=20 and S1 rollout_n=8 complete 100 steps; S3 branch=8/beam=1/rollout_n=24 fails during the Actor→vLLM synchronization phase at step 33, with the log recording only the first 32 complete timing steps. 4.3 Root Cause

Compared to S3 n=20, the initial n=24 run's mean token count over the first 32 steps is 23.6% higher, actor update time is 27.5% higher, and the fraction of non-aborted response slots rises from 36.9% to 54.9%. At the 33rd batch, S3 n=20 reaches 49,190 tokens and 50.52GiB actor reserved; S1 reaches 47,716 tokens and 49.84GiB reserved. Both complete that step. The initial n=24 run OOMs before writing out the step-33 metrics; the 44.21GiB reserved value in the log is from step 32.

The fixed rerun fills in the missing step-33 data: that step actually reaches 61,558 tokens, with an unbalanced longest-rank value of 31,884 tokens and a per-GPU balanced value of 15,394 tokens; actor allocated/reserved high-water marks are 52.37/57.54GiB respectively, and update_actor takes 26.77 seconds. After the cache fix, update_weights takes 6.16 seconds, and training continues through step 100.

At step 33, the training-synchronization peak approaches 76.0GB, leaving about 5.5GB free. The failed run uses about 74.5GB before OOM, leaving about 7GB free. vLLM TP4 subsequently needs to re-map its fixed weight shards. max_num_seqs=8 and max_num_batched_tokens=8192 only constrain generation-phase scheduling concurrency and the KV cache; they do not change the fixed weight-mapping capacity required by wake_up(weights).

The OOM chain is: the n=24 batch raises the actor's memory peak; the PyTorch allocator retains already- freed reserved blocks; physical memory is insufficient when vLLM wake-up re-maps the weights; CuMem's create_and_map fails.

Subsequent runs record per-rank memory_allocated, memory_reserved, and mem_get_info, supplementing the memory difference before and after the cache cleanup.
